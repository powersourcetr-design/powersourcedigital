A customer buying online cannot pick the product up. Everything they would have learned by handling it has to come from your photographs, and if it does not, the gap gets filled with hesitation.

That is the whole job of product photography: not to look expensive, but to answer the questions a customer would have asked in a shop. Our design and store work is on the [e-commerce development](route:landingEcommerce) page.

## The shots that answer real questions

**The main shot.** Clean background, product filling the frame, no props competing for attention. This is the one that appears in search results and category grids, so it does more work than any other.

**Angles.** Two or three, showing the sides a customer would turn the product to see.

**Scale.** The single most underrated shot. "How big is it actually?" is the question that stops a purchase, and a photograph with a familiar object or a hand in it answers it instantly where a dimensions table does not.

**In use.** Worn, installed, on a desk, in a kitchen. This is what converts, because it lets someone picture owning it.

**Detail.** Only where the detail matters — a finish, a stitch, a connector, a material.

## Consistency beats quality

A catalogue where every product is lit the same way, framed the same way and sized the same way looks professional even when the individual photographs are ordinary. A catalogue of beautiful photographs at different scales, on different backgrounds, looks like a marketplace stall.

If you are shooting yourself, fix the setup once — same position, same light, same distance — and do not move it between batches. That single decision does more for a store than better equipment.

## Marketplaces have rules, and enforce them quietly

Amazon.sa and Noon both specify background, framing and minimum dimensions, and a listing that breaks them can be suppressed without a clear explanation. Their rules are not identical to each other, and neither matches what looks best on your own store.

That is why marketplace imagery is prepared separately rather than exported — the same reason the listings themselves are, covered in [selling on Saudi marketplaces](route:blogPost/amazon-sa-vs-noon-marketplaces).

## Photographs are also a speed problem

Images are the most common reason a product page is slow, and a slow product page loses buyers before they see any of this work. Upload something large enough to zoom, then let the store serve a compressed, correctly sized version — most platforms do this if you let them.

What breaks it is uploading a 5MB original and displaying it at 600 pixels wide. [What a slow website costs you](route:blogPost/website-speed-core-web-vitals) covers why that matters more than it sounds.

## Where photos and text meet

Photographs answer what something looks like. They cannot answer what it does, what it fits, or why yours is worth more than the identical listing next to it. That is the description's job — and a description copied from your supplier, which every competitor also received, undoes good photography by making the page interchangeable with theirs.
