A marketplace gives you something your own store cannot buy cheaply: people already there, already searching, already holding a saved card. What it takes in exchange is a cut of every order and the customer relationship itself.

That trade is usually worth making. What is not worth doing is treating the two big platforms as one channel — they are not, and the difference is where most sellers lose money. If you are also building your own store, [what an online store costs](route:blogPost/ecommerce-website-cost-saudi-arabia) covers that side.

## Where they differ

**Amazon.sa** has a stricter catalogue. Titles follow a prescribed structure, attributes are validated on upload, and a listing that breaks the rules is suppressed rather than corrected. Its search rewards conversion history, which makes the first weeks of a new listing disproportionately important.

**Noon** is more forgiving on listing structure and generally easier to get live on, with strong recognition among Saudi shoppers. Category dynamics differ noticeably from Amazon's, so a product that struggles on one can do well on the other.

Neither answer holds across every category. The only reliable method is to look at how your specific category behaves on each — what is already ranking, at what price, with what content quality.

## The mistake almost everyone makes

**Exporting your website listings into a marketplace feed.** It looks efficient and it is why so many seller accounts sit with half their catalogue suppressed and nobody quite sure why.

Marketplace listings are their own content type:

- **Titles** follow a format — typically brand, product, key attribute, size — not the marketing name you use on your site
- **Attributes** are mandatory and validated. A missing one can block the listing entirely, and a wrong one puts you in the wrong filter
- **Images** have strict requirements on background, framing and minimum dimensions
- **Descriptions** written for your own store rarely survive the different formatting rules

Preparing these separately is slower once, and cheaper than a quarter of suppressed listings. It is the same discipline good product content needs anywhere: written for the buyer rather than inherited from the supplier.

## Fulfilment changes the maths

Both platforms offer their own fulfilment, and both make your product more visible when you use it. Both also charge storage, which turns slow-moving stock into a running cost rather than a one-off.

Fast-moving items usually justify it. Slow ones frequently do not, and the storage fee is the part sellers forget when they model the decision.

## Pricing is visible to everyone

Marketplaces are price-transparent in a way your own store is not. A customer sees you next to four competitors selling the same thing, sorted in a way you do not control.

That has a consequence worth planning for: if you sell the same product on your own store, decide deliberately what the relationship between the two prices is. Undercutting your own marketplace listing looks clever and tends to damage your position there.

## How to start

Pick one platform and one narrow set of products. Get the listings genuinely right — full attributes, correct images, real descriptions — and watch what happens for a month before expanding. Sellers who list everything everywhere on day one usually end up fixing all of it at once.
