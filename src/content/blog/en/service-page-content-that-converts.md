Most service pages are written from the inside out. They open with how long the company has been established, describe the team's commitment to quality, and mention the service somewhere in the third paragraph.

The visitor arrived with a question. Every sentence that does not move towards answering it is a reason to leave, and they leave silently. How we approach this is on the [web design](route:landingWebDesign) page.

## The question the page has to answer

Someone landing on your service page wants to know four things, in roughly this order:

1. **Do you do the specific thing I need?**
2. **What exactly do I get?**
3. **Roughly what does it cost?**
4. **What happens if I get in touch?**

Almost everything else is optional. A page that answers those four clearly beats a beautifully written one that circles them.

## Scope beats adjectives

"Professional web design services delivered to the highest standard" tells a buyer nothing, because every competitor says it and none of it can be checked.

A list of what is actually included — pages, revisions, who writes the content, what happens after launch — tells them everything. Specifics are also the only part a competitor cannot copy without matching your offer.

## Publish a price, or a range

This is the most common disagreement we have with clients, and the reasoning is worth setting out.

Buyers filter on budget whether you help them or not. Without a number they either guess — usually wrong in one direction or the other — or they leave to find someone who did publish. The enquiries you lose to a published price are mostly the ones that would not have closed anyway, and the ones you keep arrive already knowing the range.

A starting figure or a band is enough. [What a website costs in Saudi Arabia](route:blogPost/website-design-cost-saudi-arabia) is the same argument at length.

## Say what you will not do

A page that only sells reads like every other page. Naming the cases where your service is the wrong answer does something no amount of persuasion does: it makes the rest of the page credible.

It also filters. The person who reads "this is not for you if…" and leaves was never going to be a good project.

## Answer the objection on the page

Every service has two or three things a buyer worries about but may not ask: what happens if it takes longer, who owns the work, what if they are not happy with the design.

Those questions get asked in the sales call anyway, so answering them on the page costs nothing and removes a reason to hesitate. This is what an FAQ section is genuinely for — not keyword stuffing, but the things people actually ask.

## One page per service

A combined "Our Services" page cannot rank for any of the specific things people search, and it forces every visitor to scroll past four services to find theirs.

Give each service its own page with its own scope, its own price, and its own questions. Then link them to each other so a visitor who picked the wrong one can move sideways rather than back out — the same reasoning behind [when to redesign a website](route:blogPost/website-redesign-when-to-rebuild).

## Both languages, written not translated

A service page translated word-for-word from English keeps English sentence rhythm and reads like a form to an Arabic reader. It is the single fastest way to lose a Saudi buyer's confidence — covered in [six mistakes that make an Arabic site look translated](route:blogPost/arabic-website-design-mistakes).

## The test

Give your service page to someone outside the business and ask what they would get for their money and roughly what it would cost. If they cannot answer, the page is not finished, however good it looks.
